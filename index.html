Community volunteering in Australia

Australia’s Volunteers: Who, Where and Why

Millions of Australians coach junior sport, run op-shops, fight bushfires and check on neighbours, all without pay. But fewer of us volunteer each year, and some communities give far more time than others.

01

Fewer of us are volunteering

Formal volunteering has fallen by more than a third since 2010.

Formal volunteering means giving time through an organisation, such as a sports club, school or charity. Informal volunteering means helping people outside your household directly, such as minding a neighbour's children.

The decline began well before COVID-19, but lockdowns closed clubs and events, and many older volunteers did not return. Informal help has held up better.

2010–2025 surveys

Source: ABS General Social Survey 2010–2025, via Volunteering Australia (2026).

The volunteering rate fell in every state and territory between 2020 and 2025.

Tasmania and the Northern Territory still have the highest rates. Canberra had the steepest fall: it went from the highest rate in the country in 2020 (30.9%) to below the national average in 2025 (22.0%).

02

Where the volunteers are

Two sources, two scales. The 2021 Census asked everyone one question, so it can compare all 544 council areas. The 2025 survey asks fewer people more detailed questions, so its rates are higher and more recent but only available for states. Each chart is labelled with its source.

2021 Census

Country towns volunteer at two to three times the rate of big cities

Share of people aged 15+ who did unpaid work for an organisation or group in the past year, by local council area. Hover over a council for details, or highlight one state.

Farming shires lead. In Mount Marshall (WA) more than half of adults volunteer, and Kimba (SA) is close behind. Small towns rely on locals to run the footy club, the fire brigade and the hall.

Growing suburbs lag. In outer suburbs such as Blacktown in Sydney, fewer than 1 in 10 volunteer. Long commutes and young families leave little spare time.

Read with care. The Census was held on 10 August 2021, during lockdowns in Sydney and Melbourne. It also only counts volunteering for an organisation, so it misses help given within families and communities.

Source: ABS Census of Population and Housing 2021, table G23; boundaries: ABS Local Government Areas 2021 (ASGS Ed. 3).

High rates in the country, but most volunteers live in the cities.

The map above shows the share of people who volunteer. This map shows the number of volunteers. Big city councils have the largest circles because they have the most people, even though a smaller share of residents volunteer.

Charities need large numbers of volunteers, which cities can supply. Small towns depend on a high share of locals turning up.

03

Who gives their time

Volunteering peaks after retirement in almost every state.

Teenagers volunteer through school and sport. Rates dip at 25–34, the years of study, first jobs and young children. They rise again when children start school, and peak at 65–74, when many people have retired but are still active.

The outlined 65–74 column is the highest, or equal highest, in every state except Western Australia, where teenagers and 45–54 year olds volunteer slightly more. The darkest cells belong to Canberrans and Tasmanians aged 65–74.

New South Wales and Victoria, both in lockdown on Census night, are the palest rows.

2021 Census

Women volunteer more than men at almost every age

% of men and women in each age group who volunteered for an organisation. Both sides use the same scale, starting from zero in the centre.

Source: ABS Census 2021, table G23 (all of Australia).

1.6 million women volunteered, compared with 1.3 million men.

The gap is widest at 35–44, when many parents help at their children's schools and sports clubs. Men only catch up after 75.

Having a job does not stop people volunteering.

Part-time workers have long been among the most likely to volunteer, but their formal volunteering fell sharply after 2020.

Formal volunteering by unemployed people also dropped, from 26.3% to 12.2%. The 2020 figure is shown as a hollow dot because the ABS warns it has a high margin of error: few unemployed people were surveyed.

That rises to 40% of male volunteers, compared with 28% of women. Women are more likely to volunteer for community groups, schools, children's groups and health causes.

Religious groups are the second-largest cause for both men and women.

So what can we do?

Australia's volunteers are fewer in number and older on average, but they give more hours than ever. Country towns show what strong local volunteering looks like, while cities hold most of the untapped potential.

Short, flexible roles that fit around work and family could bring back the people who stepped away during the pandemic. The simplest step is often the most effective: ask someone to help.
